How to Choose a Card Reader for a Small Business

The right reader depends on where you stand when you sell. Match the device to your movement, volume, and customers.

PayPilot EditorialOctober 9, 20267 min read

A card reader is one of the few pieces of equipment that every customer touches. It sits at the point where a sale becomes real, so a poor choice shows up as slow lines, awkward moments, and staff who dislike the checkout.

The market can look confusing, with devices that look nothing alike. In reality, most options fall into three families, and a handful of questions will tell you which family fits.

This guide describes each type, the trade-offs that actually matter, and a checklist you can run through before you buy or switch.

Quick takeaways

  • Three families cover most needs: countertop, handheld, and pocket readers.
  • Choose by how and where you stand when the customer pays.
  • Insist on chip, tap, and phone-wallet acceptance with automatic updates.
  • Keep a backup option for the day your main device fails.

Family one: the countertop terminal

A countertop terminal stays at your register. It usually has a large screen, a keypad or touchscreen for PINs, and slots for chip and swipe plus a contactless zone. It plugs into power and uses a wired or Wi-Fi connection, which makes it steady and predictable.

This type suits shops where customers come to a fixed spot: boutiques, salons, convenience counters, and offices. The PayPilot Terminal sits in this category. The larger screen is easier for customers to read, and the stable connection means fewer surprises during a rush.

Durability deserves a line on your list. Counters in busy kitchens, salons, and workshops are hard on devices, with splashes, grease, and dropped cables. Ask about screen toughness, port covers, and whether cables are replaceable, because a reader that survives one clumsy shift is worth more than a flashier model that cracks.

Family two: the handheld with a printer

A handheld looks like a rugged smartphone with built-in card slots and often a receipt printer. It moves with you, which changes the way service works. A server can take payment at the table, a delivery driver at the door, and a vendor at a stall.

The PayPilot Go belongs here. Battery life, screen brightness, and the quality of the cellular or Wi-Fi connection matter more than for a counter device, so ask about those details first. Handhelds shine in restaurants, mobile services, and busy events.

Family three: the pocket reader

A pocket reader is a small accessory that pairs with a phone or tablet. It is inexpensive to carry, light enough for a jacket pocket, and a natural fit for low-volume or part-time selling. The PayPilot Reader fills this role.

The trade-off is that you rely on a second device for the screen, the app, and the connection. A dead phone means a dead checkout, so carry a charger. It is a fine starter, and also a handy backup beside a main terminal.

Questions that narrow the choice

Skip the spec sheet at first and think about your day. Where do you stand when the customer is ready to pay, and how many people are in line behind them?

Run through these questions honestly:

  • Does the sale happen at a fixed spot or all over the room?
  • Do customers need privacy to type a PIN?
  • Do you hand out printed receipts, or are digital ones enough?
  • How many sales do you expect during your busiest hour?
  • What is the internet situation where you sell?
  • How many staff members will use the device each shift?

Features worth insisting on

Whatever the family, a few capabilities are close to non-negotiable today. The device should accept chip, tap, and phone wallets such as Apple Pay and Google Pay, since many customers never touch their physical card. It should receive software and security updates automatically and encrypt card data from the moment it is read.

Beyond that, think about the extras you will actually use, such as a customer-facing screen for tip selection, a built-in printer, or a cash drawer connection. Extras you will not use are just clutter and cost.

Staff comfort matters too. A device with a screen too small for tired eyes or a keypad that sticks slows everyone down. Let employees hold the candidates, run a few practice taps, and tell you which one feels natural, since they will be the ones using it ten hours a week.

Placement and ergonomics

Where you put the device changes how fast people pay. Keep it within easy reach of the customer, angled so they can read the screen without leaning, and away from glare. A device that requires a customer to stretch across the counter slows everyone down.

For PIN privacy, make sure the keypad is not visible to the next person in line. A small rotation or a shield can make customers feel safer and cut the number of mistyped PINs.

A simple decision checklist

Before buying, test the experience if you can. Hold the device, tap a card, and see how quickly it responds. Ask how support works and what happens if the device fails, since downtime can cost more than the hardware.

FAQ

Which reader is best for a brand-new business?

A pocket reader or phone-based setup keeps early costs low while you learn your volume. If you already know you will have a fixed counter, a countertop terminal may serve you better from the start. Revisit the choice after a few months of real sales data.

Do I need a reader that takes chip cards?

Yes. Chip acceptance is standard, and it protects you better than swipe-only acceptance when a card is counterfeit. Look for a device that also supports contactless taps and phone wallets so you can serve every customer without friction. Ask for a trial period if one is offered.

Can one reader work for several employees?

Yes, as long as each employee signs in with their own login. That lets you track who processed each sale and limit sensitive actions such as refunds. Shared logins make mistakes harder to trace, so avoid them. Pick the model that fits your counter, not the longest feature list.

What if my internet connection is unreliable?

Choose a device that can switch between Wi-Fi and cellular, and consider a handheld with its own data plan. Test the signal at your actual selling spot. Ask about offline options and their limits, since stored payments carry extra risk.

Is a receipt printer necessary?

Not always. Many customers prefer a text or email receipt. Restaurants, retailers with returns, and mobile services often like having paper available. If you are unsure, start digital and add a printer later if customers ask for it. Ask for a trial period if one is offered.
